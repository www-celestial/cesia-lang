# 3D objects

In this chapter you draw solid objects in 3D, as a CAD program shows them: parts made of triangles, lit by a light,
shaded, with the edges of a drawing, the near parts hiding the far ones, seen from far away or through a camera. You
make the parts with [std/mesh], boxes, cylinders, cones and spheres moved and turned into place, or read them from an
STL file, and [plot.Mesh] draws them in a graph of [std/plot], saved as SVG or shown under a cell of Cemacs, which turns
them with the mouse. Step by step, you build a small machine. The last sections say how each step works, with the
papers it follows, its limits, and how matplotlib, MATLAB, plotly and PyVista do the same.

You need nothing of 3D to start: [Graphs](graphs.md) is enough, and its section [3D](graphs.md#3d) shows the view, the
box and the axes that these graphs share.

> [!NOTE]
> Some blocks have a tab with the same graph in Python, with matplotlib, which runs when the book is checked. They have
> no MATLAB tab: GNU Octave, which runs the MATLAB tabs of the book, draws no graph without a window. The MATLAB names of
> each step are given in the text.

## One box, lit

[mesh.Box] makes a box from a corner and its size along x, y and z. [plot.Mesh] makes a layer of it, which [plot.Of]
takes as any layer, and [plot.Save] writes the SVG:

```cesia allow=read,write
use std/mesh
use std/plot
use std/value
block = mesh.Box([0, 0, 0], [40, 20, 10])
plot.Save(plot.Of([plot.Mesh(block)], title = "A box of 40 by 20 by 10"), "box.svg") -->
```

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d.art3d import Poly3DCollection
corners = np.array([[x, y, z] for z in (0, 10) for y in (0, 20) for x in (0, 40)], dtype=float)
faces = [[0, 2, 3, 1], [4, 5, 7, 6], [0, 1, 5, 4], [1, 3, 7, 5], [3, 2, 6, 7], [2, 0, 4, 6]]
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(6.4, 4.0))
ax = fig.add_subplot(projection="3d")
ax.add_collection3d(Poly3DCollection([corners[f] for f in faces], facecolors="#b0b4b8", shade=True))
ax.set(xlim=(0, 40), ylim=(0, 20), zlim=(0, 10), title="A box of 40 by 20 by 10")
ax.set_box_aspect((40, 20, 10))
ax.view_init(elev=30, azim=-127.5)
fig.savefig("box.svg")
```

The box is light grey, as metal, and each of its three sides that face you has its own shade: the top, which faces the
light, is the brightest. That is all a 3D picture needs to look solid. The graph is seen from MATLAB's view,
`[-37.5, 30]`, and a unit is as long on each axis, so that the box keeps its shape: 40 long, 20 deep and 10 high.

A *mesh* is a value of two matrices. `Vertices` holds the points, a row x, y, z for each; `Triangles` holds the
triangles, a row for each, the rows of its three corners among the points, from 1:

```cesia
block.Vertices -->
value.Size(block.Triangles) -->
block.Triangles[1, :] -->
```

A box has 8 points and 12 triangles, two on each side. The corners of each triangle turn counterclockwise seen from
outside, so that the right hand's rule, the fingers along the corners in their order, points the thumb out of the part:
the *normal* of the triangle, which says which way it faces. Every function of [std/mesh] keeps that order, as the STL
format asks, and [plot.Mesh] uses it to know the outside of a part. It is the form of MATLAB's `triangulation`, with
`Points` and `ConnectivityList`, and of the `faces` and `vertices` of its `patch`.

## The light

Without a `light`, the light comes from your eye, turned 30 degrees up, as a lamp fixed above a camera: the faces turned
to you are lit, the tops the most, and the light follows the view when the graph turns. `light = [x, y, z]` gives the
direction the light comes from, fixed along the axes, as the sun is fixed while you walk around a house:

```cesia allow=read,write
lights = plot.Grid([plot.Of([plot.Mesh(block)], light = [1, -1, 2], title = "light = [1, -1, 2]"),
  plot.Of([plot.Mesh(block)], light = [-2, -1, 0.5], title = "light = [-2, -1, 0.5]")])
plot.Save(lights, "lights.svg") -->
```

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LightSource
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d.art3d import Poly3DCollection
corners = np.array([[x, y, z] for z in (0, 10) for y in (0, 20) for x in (0, 40)], dtype=float)
faces = [[0, 2, 3, 1], [4, 5, 7, 6], [0, 1, 5, 4], [1, 3, 7, 5], [3, 2, 6, 7], [2, 0, 4, 6]]
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12.8, 4.0))
for k, light in enumerate([(1, -1, 2), (-2, -1, 0.5)]):
    x, y, z = light
    # LightSource takes the azimuth clockwise from north, +y, and the altitude above the plane, in degrees.
    ls = LightSource(azdeg=np.degrees(np.arctan2(x, y)) % 360, altdeg=np.degrees(np.arctan2(z, np.hypot(x, y))))
    ax = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, k + 1, projection="3d")
    ax.add_collection3d(Poly3DCollection([corners[f] for f in faces], facecolors="#b0b4b8", shade=True, lightsource=ls))
    ax.set(xlim=(0, 40), ylim=(0, 20), zlim=(0, 10), title=f"light = [{x}, {y}, {z}]")
    ax.set_box_aspect((40, 20, 10))
    ax.view_init(elev=30, azim=-127.5)
fig.savefig("lights.svg")
```

On the left the light comes from the front, the right and above: the top is bright, the front a little less, and the
left side, which turns away from it, is dark. On the right it comes low from the left: the left side is the bright one.

The shade of a face follows three terms, the model of Phong (1975) as Blinn (1977) writes its highlight. With $n$ the
normal of the face, $l$ the direction of the light and $e$ the direction of the eye, all of length 1, and $h$ the
direction halfway between $l$ and $e$:

$$\text{color} \times (0.3 + 0.7 \max(0, n \cdot l)) + \text{white} \times 0.2 \max(0, n \cdot h)^{20}$$

- 0.3, the *ambient* light, comes from all around: a face in the shadow keeps 30 % of its color, so that it never turns
  black and its shape still shows.
- $0.7 \max(0, n \cdot l)$ is the *diffuse* light, Lambert's law of 1760: a matte surface is as bright as the cosine of
  the angle between its normal and the light, all of it when it faces the light, none when the light grazes it.
- The last term is the *highlight*, the white spot that a polished surface shows where it mirrors the light toward the
  eye: brightest when $n$ is $h$, and narrow, as the power 20 makes it. It adds at most a fifth of white.

Each face is one color, its *flat shading*: the faces of a part show as the facets of a CAD part, and a cylinder of 48
sides shows its sides. A light of length 0 has no direction, and is an error:

```cesia
plot.Svg(plot.Of([plot.Mesh(block)], light = [0, 0, 0])) -->
```

MATLAB lights a `patch` with `light` or `camlight`, `camlight` alone a light above and right of the camera, and
`lighting flat`; matplotlib's `Poly3DCollection` and `plot_trisurf` shade with a `LightSource`.

## Edges

A drawing of a part shows its edges: where two faces meet at an angle, and its outline. `edges = true` draws them in a
darker shade of the part's color:

```cesia allow=read,write
shaft = mesh.Cylinder([0, 0, 0], 5, 30)
value.Size(shaft.Triangles) -->
both = plot.Grid([plot.Of([plot.Mesh(shaft)], title = "edges = false"), plot.Of([plot.Mesh(shaft, edges = true)],
  title = "edges = true")])
plot.Save(both, "shaft.svg") -->
```

[mesh.Cylinder] stands on the center of its base, along z: here a radius of 5 and a height of 30. Its side is made of
48 rectangles, two triangles each, and each end of 48 triangles around its center: 192 triangles. Three kinds of edges
are drawn:

- the *feature edges*, where two faces meet at more than 30 degrees, as VTK finds them: the rim of each end, where the
  side meets the end at 90 degrees. Two sides of the cylinder meet at 7.5 degrees, and their edge isn't drawn, so that
  the side looks round;
- the *outline*, the edges between a face turned to the eye and one turned away: the two long lines on each side of the
  cylinder, which move as it turns;
- the *border* of a part that isn't closed, an edge of one triangle, as the edge of a sheet.

An edge is hidden where a nearer face covers it, as a face is: the far half of the bottom rim doesn't show.

## Perspective and the view

A graph is seen from far away by default, an *orthographic* projection: lines that are parallel in space stay parallel
on the screen, which is how a technical drawing measures. `perspective = true` draws what a camera sees, the near parts
larger than the far ones. `axes = false` leaves out the box, its grid and its ticks, as a CAD program shows a part
alone:

```cesia allow=read,write
bar = mesh.Box([0, 0, 0], [120, 10, 10])
seen = (p, name) -> plot.Of([plot.Mesh(bar, edges = true)], view = [-20, 25], perspective = p, axes = false, title = name)
plot.Save(plot.Grid([seen(false, "perspective = false"), seen(true, "perspective = true")]), "bar.svg") -->
```

The bar is 120 long and seen along its length: through the camera, its near end is larger and its far end smaller. The
camera is a *pinhole camera*, the simplest model of one (Hartley and Zisserman, chapter 6): a point is drawn where the
line from it to the eye crosses the screen, so that its size on the screen is divided by its distance. The eye is 4
times the radius of the sphere around the box away from its middle, and the scale keeps the whole box inside the graph
at every view.

`view = [az, el]` turns the graph, as in [Graphs](graphs.md#the-view): the azimuth `az` around the vertical axis, 0
looking from the side of $-y$, and the elevation `el`, from $-90$ below to $90$ above. A graph with a mesh draws a unit
as long on each axis, `aspect = "equal"`, as a part must be drawn; `aspect = "box"` stretches the data to the box of 1 by
1 by 0.75 of a surface. In MATLAB they are `camproj perspective`, `view`, `axis equal` and `axis off`; in matplotlib
`ax.set_proj_type("persp")`, `ax.view_init`, `ax.set_box_aspect` and `ax.set_axis_off()`.

## Moving, turning and joining

A part is made where it is easiest, at 0, then moved into place. [mesh.Move] moves it by `[dx, dy, dz]`, [mesh.Turn]
turns it by an angle in degrees around an axis, [mesh.Scale] scales it, and [mesh.Bounds] gives the box that holds it,
which tells where it went:

```cesia
wheel = mesh.Turn(mesh.Cylinder([0, 0, 0], 26, 10), [0, 1, 0], 90)
mesh.Bounds(wheel) -->
mesh.Bounds(mesh.Move(wheel, [65, 30, 36])) -->
mesh.Bounds(mesh.Scale(block, 0.5)) -->
```

The cylinder stood along z; turned by 90 degrees around the axis y, it lies along x: a wheel 52 across and 10 thick. A
positive angle turns counterclockwise seen from the tip of the axis, the right hand's rule, as MATLAB's `rotate`. The
turn goes around the point `about`, `[0, 0, 0]` by default, and the formula of Rodrigues gives each point. A mesh is a
value: each function gives a new one, and `block` is as it was.

[mesh.Merge] joins meshes into one, whose points and triangles are those of each, one after the other:

```cesia
value.Size(mesh.Merge([block, mesh.Move(block, [0, 0, 10])]).Triangles) -->
```

A merged mesh is drawn in one color, and is one part for the order of the parts below; give each part of a machine its
own [plot.Mesh] instead.

## A small machine

Now a machine: a base, two bearing blocks, a shaft through them, a wheel on the shaft and a knob at its end, the metal
grey and the plastic green, as a CAD program shows a machine:

```cesia allow=read,write
metal = "#b0b4b8"
green = "#3cb043"
base = mesh.Box([0, 0, 0], [220, 60, 8])
left = mesh.Box([20, 15, 8], [16, 30, 40])
right = mesh.Move(left, [90, 0, 0])
axle = mesh.Move(mesh.Turn(mesh.Cylinder([0, 0, 0], 5, 150), [0, 1, 0], 90), [5, 30, 36])
pulley = mesh.Move(wheel, [65, 30, 36])
knob = mesh.Move(mesh.Turn(mesh.Cone([0, 0, 0], 9, 14), [0, 1, 0], -90), [5, 30, 36])
machine = plot.Of([plot.Mesh(base, color = metal, edges = true), plot.Mesh(left, color = green, edges = true),
  plot.Mesh(right, color = green, edges = true), plot.Mesh(axle, color = metal, edges = true),
  plot.Mesh(pulley, color = metal, edges = true), plot.Mesh(knob, color = green, edges = true)], axes = false,
  title = "A shaft on two bearings, with its wheel")
plot.Save(machine, "machine.svg") -->
```

`color` is written `"#rrggbb"`, red, green and blue from `00` to `ff`. [mesh.Cone] stands on its base along z, as the
cylinder; turned by $-90$ degrees around y, its tip points to $-x$, out of the end of the shaft.

The shaft goes through both blocks and the wheel. Each part is drawn whole, and the parts are set in order, far first,
by a plane between them: a part on the far side of a plane is painted before the one on the near side. Where no plane
sets two parts apart, because one goes through the other, the shaft is cut by the planes of the faces of the block it
crosses, and what of it is inside the block is left out: what remains on each side of the block is a piece apart, set in
order by the face it lies past. So the shaft shows where it comes out of each block and of the wheel, and nowhere else,
at every view. [How it works](#hidden-parts) says when it fails.

## A part from an STL file

A CAD program exports a part as an *STL* file, the format that 3D Systems defined in 1988 for its stereolithography
machines and that every CAD program and 3D printer reads: a list of triangles, each with its normal and its three
corners. `support.stl`, among the files of this book, is an angle bracket, an L of 8 mm plates with a rounded inside
corner, exported by another program. [mesh.ReadStl] reads it:

```cesia allow=read,write
use std/result
bracket = result.Unwrap(mesh.ReadStl("support.stl"))
value.Size(bracket.Triangles) -->
mesh.Bounds(bracket) -->
```

88 triangles, 50 long, 30 deep and 45 high. Turned by 180 degrees around z and moved, it stands on the base, at the end
of the shaft. A [plot.Of] of the machine and of the bracket joins their layers, as for any graph:

```cesia allow=read,write
placed = mesh.Move(mesh.Turn(bracket, [0, 0, 1], 180), [215, 45, 8])
mesh.Bounds(placed) -->
whole = plot.Of([machine, plot.Mesh(placed, color = green, edges = true)], axes = false,
  title = "The machine and its bracket")
plot.Save(whole, "machine-bracket.svg") -->
```

STL comes in two forms. The *binary* one has a header of 80 bytes, the count of triangles in 4 bytes, then 50 bytes for
each triangle: its normal and its corners, 12 floats of 32 bits, and 2 bytes that most programs leave at 0. The *text*
one writes `facet normal`, `outer loop`, three `vertex` lines, `endloop` and `endfacet` for each. [mesh.ReadStl] reads
both, makes one point of the corners at one place, which a file repeats for each triangle, and leaves the normals of
the file aside, as most readers do: the order of the corners gives them. [mesh.WriteStl] writes a mesh, binary unless
`ascii = true`; read back, it is the same mesh, as its numbers are floats of 32 bits:

```cesia allow=read,write
mesh.WriteStl(bracket, "copy.stl") -->
value.Equal(result.Unwrap(mesh.ReadStl("copy.stl")), bracket) -->
```

A file from anyone is checked before anything is made of it. A binary file must be exactly $84 + 50n$ bytes for the
$n$ triangles its header counts, so that a count that lies is an error and never a memory taken; a text must follow the
grammar of facets; a coordinate NaN or infinite, or more than 2,000,000 triangles, 100 MB, is an error. Here a CSV file
read as STL:

```cesia
mesh.ReadStl("sales.csv") -->
```

Under `--limit memory=`, a part too large for the limit stops its fiber with an error that [fiber.Wait] gives, and the
program goes on. MATLAB reads STL with `stlread`, which gives a `triangulation`, and writes it with `stlwrite`; Python
has the packages `numpy-stl` and `trimesh`.

## A lit surface

A [plot.Surface] of [Graphs](graphs.md#3d) is colored by its height, without light, and stays so: an SVG of a surface
is the same bytes as before. Given a `light`, or `perspective = true`, its quads are shaded too, with the normal of each
quad and the same three terms:

```cesia allow=read,write
use std/math
use std/matrix
use std/num
x = num.Linspace(-3.0, 3.0, 61)
across = matrix.Matmul(matrix.Ones(61, 1), matrix.Reshape([1, 61], x))
r = math.Sqrt(across * across + matrix.Transpose(across) * matrix.Transpose(across))
ripple = plot.Surface(math.Sin(2.0 * r) / (1.0 + r), x = x, y = x)
lit = plot.Of([ripple], light = [1, -1, 2], perspective = true, title = "A ripple, lit, through a pinhole")
plot.Save(lit, "ripple-lit.svg") -->
```

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.colors import LightSource
x = np.linspace(-3.0, 3.0, 61)
gx, gy = np.meshgrid(x, x)
r = np.sqrt(gx * gx + gy * gy)
z = np.sin(2.0 * r) / (1.0 + r)
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(6.4, 4.0))
ax = fig.add_subplot(projection="3d")
colors = LightSource(azdeg=135, altdeg=54.7).shade(z, cmap=plt.cm.viridis, blend_mode="soft")
ax.plot_surface(gx, gy, z, facecolors=colors, rstride=1, cstride=1, linewidth=0, antialiased=False, shade=False)
ax.set_proj_type("persp")
ax.view_init(elev=30, azim=-127.5)
ax.set_title("A ripple, lit, through a pinhole")
fig.savefig("ripple-lit.svg")
```

The color still says the height, and the light shows the slopes: the ring around the middle is bright on its side
toward the light and dark on the other. A surface seen through a pinhole needs another order of its quads than the one
of [Graphs](graphs.md#the-order-of-the-quads): the order of McMillan (1995) for a projection from a point, the rows from
the farthest from the foot of the eye on the grid, which keeps every quad behind another first.

## In Cemacs

Under a cell of Cemacs, a graph of meshes is drawn by the graphics card, from the triangles that [cells.Scene] gives: a
drag of the mouse on it turns it, as the arrows do after `C-c e f`, and each view is drawn again by the session, the light
that follows the view moving with it. The code keeps its own view. [Cells in Cemacs](https://www-celestial.github.io/cemacs/cells.html)
shows it, and a scene holds at most 400,000 triangles and 600,000 edges there.

## How it works

### The mesh

A [mesh.Mesh] holds each point once, as MATLAB's `triangulation`: a corner shared by six triangles is stored once, and
a part of $n$ triangles takes about $n / 2$ points. The primitives are closed, their triangles counterclockwise from
outside. [mesh.Sphere] is a globe: `segments` meridians and `segments / 2` bands between its poles; 48 segments, the
default of the cylinder, the cone and the sphere, put the side within 0.2 % of the circle, less than a pixel at the size
of a graph. [mesh.Normals] gives the normal of each triangle, $(b - a) \times (c - a)$ divided by its length.

### Shading

The light is that of Phong (1975), *Illumination for computer generated pictures*, in the form of Blinn (1977), *Models
of light reflection for computer synthesized pictures*: Blinn writes the highlight with $h$, halfway between the light
and the eye, $\max(0, n \cdot h)^c$, which needs no reflected ray. Its diffuse term is Lambert's cosine law, from his
*Photometria* of 1760. A face seen from behind, the inside of a sheet, is lit as its other side.

Each face takes one color. Gouraud (1971), *Continuous shading of curved surfaces*, shades smoothly instead: a color at
each point, from a normal at each point, blended across each triangle. A smooth shading makes a sphere of few triangles
look round, but it blurs the edges a part has on purpose, and SVG has no blend of three colors in a triangle: flat
shading is the look of a CAD part, and the one this version draws. The normal of a point would be the mean of the normals
of its triangles weighted by their angles at it, after Thürmer and Wüthrich (1998), *Computing vertex normals from
polygonal facets*.

### Hidden parts

A picture of 3D must hide what is behind. The *painter's algorithm* of Newell, Newell and Sancha (1972), *A solution to
the hidden surface problem*, paints from far to near, each face over those behind it. They say where it fails: faces
that cross each other, and faces that cover one another in a cycle, each partly over the next. [plot.Mesh] orders in
three steps.

1. **Faces turned away.** A closed part, each edge shared by two triangles, hides its faces turned away from the eye
   behind those turned to it: they are left out, about half of the triangles.
2. **One part.** A closed part that is convex, its edges all bent outward and its points, edges and faces counting
   $V - E + F = 2$, is a convex solid (Tietze, 1928): its faces turned to the eye never cover each other, and need no
   order. A part that isn't convex is painted by the depth of the middle of each triangle, far first, as matplotlib's
   `Poly3DCollection` does, with the faults that Newell, Newell and Sancha name.
3. **Several parts.** Two parts whose triangles may meet on the screen are set in order by a plane between them, the
   theorem of the separating hyperplane: the part on the far side of a plane through which no part passes is painted
   first. The planes tried are those of the axes, the line between their middles, and the faces of a convex part. Two
   convex parts that cross have no such plane: one is cut by the planes of the faces of the other, as Newell, Newell and
   Sancha cut a face by the plane of another, and its pieces inside the other are left out. Each piece left lies past a
   plane of the other part, which sets it apart. Cutting one part is enough: a face of the other inside it, turned to
   the eye, is seen through the part cut, through a piece on the eye's side of that plane, painted after it. The part
   cut is the one that the fewer planes of the other cross: the shaft by the two faces of a block, not the block by the
   48 sides of the shaft. The pieces are made once, when [plot.Of] makes the graph, as no view changes them.

A test draws random parts that cross, a ball and a block through it, a wheel, a shaft and a plate, at random views, and
checks at points of the screen that the part painted last is the one that a ray from the eye meets first, computed apart
from [std/plot] by the planes of the faces of each part. It tries 12 scenes in each run of the tests; 600 scenes, at 100
points each, gave no failure.

### Edges

Two faces meet at a *dihedral angle*; their normals at the same angle. An edge of a drawing is one whose normals differ by
more than 30 degrees, $n_1 \cdot n_2 < \cos 30^\circ$, the test and the default of VTK's `vtkFeatureEdges`. The outline is an
edge between a face turned to the eye and one turned away, found again at each view. Each edge is drawn just after the
later of its two faces in the painting, so that a nearer face painted after covers it as it covers the face.

## Limits

- A graph holds at most 200,000 triangles in all; an SVG takes about 70 bytes for each triangle drawn, half of those of
  a closed part, so 3.4 MB for a ball of 100,000. Past it, [plot.Mesh] or [plot.Of] says so in an Err. A depth buffer on
  a graphics card draws millions; SVG paints each triangle in a browser that has no depth buffer.
- Two parts that cross are drawn right when both are convex, as boxes, cylinders, cones and spheres are, and their cut is
  at most 2,000,000 steps of work, the planes times the triangles cut; a part that isn't convex and crosses another is
  painted by the depth of its triangles, and may show a face behind another near the line where they cross. A part made
  of several convex parts, given as several [plot.Mesh], is drawn right.
- Three parts that cover one another in a cycle, each over the next, are painted with the cycle broken where it waits the
  least: a corner of one may show over another. No such cycle came in the test above.
- The line where two parts meet isn't drawn as an edge; each part's own edges are.
- Shading is flat, one light, no shadow cast by one part on another, no transparency.
- Cemacs draws at most 400,000 triangles and 600,000 edges in a scene; [mesh.ReadStl] reads at most 2,000,000
  triangles.

| | Cesia | matplotlib `mplot3d` | MATLAB, Octave | plotly `Mesh3d` | PyVista, VTK |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| parts | [plot.Mesh], [std/mesh] | `plot_trisurf`, `Poly3DCollection` | `patch`, `trisurf` | `go.Mesh3d` | `pv.PolyData`, `add_mesh` |
| hidden parts | faces turned away left out, parts ordered by planes, crossing convex parts cut | polygons sorted by their mean depth, `zsort='average'` | the graphics card's depth buffer | WebGL, a depth buffer | OpenGL, a depth buffer |
| light | from the eye or `light`, ambient 0.3, diffuse 0.7, highlight 0.2 | `LightSource`, faces shaded by $n \cdot l$ into 0.3 to 1 | `light`, `camlight`; ambient 0.3, diffuse 0.6, specular 0.9 | ambient 0.8, diffuse 0.8, specular 0.05 | a light kit of 5 lights |
| shading | flat | flat | `lighting flat` or `gouraud` | smooth by default | flat by default, `smooth_shading` |
| edges | edges of a drawing, at 30° | every edge of every polygon, or none | every edge, or none | none | every edge, `show_edges`, or `extract_feature_edges` at 30° |
| STL | `ReadStl`, `WriteStl` | none; `numpy-stl` | `stlread`, `stlwrite` | none | `pv.read` |
| output | SVG, Cemacs | SVG, PNG, a window | a window, `print` | a page with plotly.js | a window, PNG |

Where they win: a depth buffer, on the graphics card of MATLAB, plotly and PyVista, is right at every pixel, whatever
crosses whatever, and draws millions of triangles as they turn; they shade smoothly, show transparency and, in PyVista,
shadows and textures. plotly turns the part in the page itself. Where Cesia wins: its SVG is exact and sharp at any zoom,
light for a page, the same in Cemacs; a part crossing another is right without a graphics card, where matplotlib's sort
by mean depth shows the hidden part; and the edges are those of a drawing, not every triangle.
